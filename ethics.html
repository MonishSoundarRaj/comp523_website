Ethics Writeups

Three case studies on professional responsibility, software behavior, and the consequences of design decisions.

Navier-Stokes priority controversy

Two mathematicians alleged that their unpublished progress on a famous fluid-dynamics problem, developed partly with OpenAI’s Codex, was leaked or absorbed into OpenAI’s own AI-generated solution announced soon after. OpenAI denies this, though it acknowledged that de-identified user data might have improved its models, so the facts remain contested.

If the allegations hold, the problem is both behavior and function: people at the company allegedly tried to cut one researcher out of authorship and made remarks Buckmaster took as a threat, while AI tools that learn from user inputs blur the line between serving customers and competing with them. Even without direct misuse, the lack of transparency about how user data feeds model development is ethically troubling.

Volkswagen emissions scandal

Volkswagen’s engines contained “defeat device” software that detected emissions testing and turned on pollution controls only during the test, so real-world driving produced far more nitrogen oxides than legally allowed. The software worked exactly as designed, so the wrongdoing lies in its deceptive purpose: misleading regulators and consumers, and harming public health, to save costs.

The problem is overwhelmingly the behavior of the engineers and managers who knowingly built, approved, or concealed it. “Following orders” is not an adequate defense for professionals who understand what they are building.

Therac-25

This radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to patients in the mid-1980s because of software race conditions and the removal of hardware safety interlocks. The function itself, treating cancer, was legitimate, so the fault lies in the engineers’ and manufacturer’s conduct.

They reused untested code, relied solely on software for safety, wrote cryptic error messages, and dismissed early injury reports instead of investigating them. The ethical failure is negligence: when software can kill, professionals have a duty to verify rigorously and respond honestly to incidents.
