An AI library guide for rural North Carolina

Public libraries across North Carolina already pay for far more than most people ever use — health databases, e-book and audiobook services like Libby and Hoopla, tutoring, job tools, streaming, and local programs. The problem is not access. The problem is that almost nobody knows these resources exist.

Library Navigator is an AI-assisted chatbot that closes that gap. A patron types a ZIP code and a plain-English question — “how do I find reliable information about managing diabetes?” — and the assistant answers with the specific resources their own library system offers, ranked by how close they are, and then walks them through getting in, step by step: which site to open, where the login is, what their library card number does, what to click next.

This matters most in rural counties, where the nearest library may be the only reliable on-ramp to health information, broadband, and study support — and where patrons are least likely to have someone to ask. It is not only patrons who are in the dark: librarians themselves often cannot name the full catalog of subscriptions their system carries. So the tool serves both sides. Patrons get a guide. Librarians get an admin panel to correct, extend, and govern the data behind it.

Why this approach

  • Grounded, not guessed. Answers come from a curated dataset of roughly 467 resource records covering all 100 NC counties, 90 library systems, and 413 outlets — not from a model improvising.
  • Guided, not just informative. Modeled on the NC DMV's AI agent, the assistant walks users through the actual access steps instead of dropping a link and leaving.
  • Honest about uncertainty. High-confidence answers are served directly. Medium- and low-confidence answers are flagged, cited, and routed to a human.
  • Built to be maintained. Librarians can add, edit, override, and retire entries, so the dataset does not rot the day the semester ends.

Scope of this semester

The deliverable is a functioning proof of concept with working code — not a clickable mockup. The first iteration uses the curated dataset as its backend. The longer-term vision, beyond this semester, is a live agent that keeps the database current on its own under librarian oversight.

Section 03

Team Rules

Agreed by the whole team. Amendments require agreement at a team meeting and are logged in the journal.

Communication

  • The team group chat is the primary channel for day-to-day coordination.
  • Messages that ask something of you get a reply within 24 hours on weekdays, even if the reply is “seen, answering tonight.”
  • Anything urgent — a broken build before a demo, a missed meeting — is a phone call, not a chat message.
  • All email to the client or the professor goes out from the Client Manager, with the rest of the team copied. Nobody emails the client solo.
  • Decisions made in chat are not decisions until they are written into the journal on this site.

Meetings

  • Attendance at the weekly team meeting, the coach meeting, and client meetings is expected.
  • If you cannot make one, say so at least 24 hours ahead and send your status in writing beforehand.
  • An agenda goes into the group chat before each meeting; notes are posted to the journal within 24 hours after.
  • Every meeting ends by naming action items, an owner for each, and a due date.
  • Come having done what you said you would, or having said in advance that you could not.

Working agreements

  • Work is divided at the weekly meeting. Taking a task means committing to a date.
  • Raise blockers within 24 hours of hitting them, not at the deadline. A blocker surfaced early is a team problem; surfaced late it is a crisis.
  • All work happens on feature branches. No direct pushes to main.
  • Every pull request needs review and approval from at least one teammate before merge.
  • Definition of done: it works, a teammate has reviewed it, it is documented, and it is merged.
  • Client data and anything the client marks confidential stays off this public site.
  • No credentials, API keys, or dataset exports in the repository.

Decisions and conflict

  • Decisions are made by consensus where possible. If the team is genuinely split, the Project Manager decides and the reasoning is recorded.
  • A decision that has been made and logged is not relitigated without new information.
  • Disagreements are raised directly with the person involved, early and in plain terms.
  • If something cannot be resolved inside the team, it goes to the coach, and then to the professor.
  • A missed commitment without notice is discussed at the next team meeting. A repeated pattern is escalated to the coach.

Non-functional requirements

IDRequirement
NFR-1Plain language. Output targets roughly a 6th–8th grade reading level. No library jargon without explanation — “database,” “catalog,” and “consortium” mean nothing to most patrons.
NFR-2Accessibility. WCAG 2.1 AA: keyboard navigable, screen-reader usable, adequate contrast, resizable text. The primary persona is 72 years old.
NFR-3Performance. First response under ~5 seconds; usable on slow rural connections; no heavy client-side payloads.
NFR-4Privacy. No account required for patrons. ZIP code only — never a street address. No personally identifying information stored. Health-topic queries are especially sensitive and are not retained against an identity.
NFR-5Bounded cost. API spend is estimated and capped; a cost estimate with justification is delivered to the client before any paid service is turned on.
NFR-6Maintainability. The dataset is swappable without a code change, so the client can replace it as it is finalized and extended.

Out of scope for this semester

  • Real-time web crawling / live RAG over library websites (e.g. Firecrawl). Discussed and deliberately deferred — see decision D-1 in the timeline.
  • The fully autonomous agent that keeps the database current on its own. That is the long-term vision, not the semester's deliverable.
  • Native iOS/Android applications. The web interface is responsive instead.
  • Languages other than English. A stretch goal at best.
  • Patron accounts, saved history, or authentication for non-librarian users.

User personas

Four personas guide design and evaluation. Every feature is checked against at least one of them.

1. Ruth, 72 — managing hypertension and type 2 diabetes. Lives outside a small town in eastern North Carolina. Her doctor is 40 minutes away and she sees them twice a year. She has a library card from a decade ago and a tablet her daughter set up. Needs: trustworthy, readable health information; help getting past a login screen; large text; no jargon. Frustrations: does not know the library offers anything online at all; abandons any site that asks her to “authenticate”; afraid of clicking the wrong thing. Success looks like: she reaches a reputable health database and reads an article about her condition without calling her daughter.

2. Danielle, 38 — homeschooling parent of three. Rural county in the northwest. Comfortable with technology, chronically short on time. Needs: pediatric health information she can trust, reading and curriculum material by grade level, e-books and audiobooks for the kids, and the county library's programs. Frustrations: cannot tell what is free through the library versus what she is about to pay for twice; resources are scattered across a dozen sites. Success looks like: one list of what her library already pays for, by subject and age, with the access steps attached.

3. Marcus, 16 — rural teenager seeking mental health support. Southeastern North Carolina. Phone-only. There is one counselor for his whole school. Needs: confidential, after-hours, mobile-first access to mental health information and local services. Frustrations: few local options; will not ask a librarian in person; does not want a record of what he searched. Success looks like: he finds real local and statewide support — and if he is in crisis, he is handed the crisis line immediately rather than a chatbot's opinion.

4. Sandra, 45 — branch librarian and system administrator. Manages a branch in the foothills. Has worked there eleven years and still finds subscriptions she did not know about. Needs: to correct wrong entries for her branch, fix broken links, add local events, and override an answer the assistant is getting wrong. Frustrations: the catalog of resources is larger than any one person can hold; nobody owns keeping the list current. Success looks like: she fixes a bad entry in under two minutes and sees the assistant's answer change.

User stories

Ruth — older adult with a chronic condition

As Ruth, I want to type my ZIP code and be told which library serves me, so that I know where to start.
Accepts: valid NC ZIP returns the serving system and nearest outlets with address and hours, nearest first.
As Ruth, I want to ask about managing my diabetes in my own words and be shown resources my library already pays for.
Accepts: results are filtered to what her system actually carries; each result names its source and links to it.
As Ruth, I want to be walked through logging in one step at a time, so that I do not get lost.
Accepts: one instruction per step; the assistant waits for confirmation before continuing; the user can say “go back.”
As Ruth, I want to be told plainly when the assistant is not sure, so that I do not act on something wrong.
Accepts: below the confidence threshold, the answer is visibly marked as unverified and the librarian contact is offered.

Danielle — homeschooling parent

As Danielle, I want to see everything my library offers for children by subject and age, so that I stop paying for things I already have.
Accepts: results can be filtered by audience and format; the access model (free with card / in-library only / paid) is stated for each.
As Danielle, I want to know how to get my kids onto Libby and Hoopla, so that I can set it up once.
Accepts: a guided walkthrough covering card number, app install, and library selection.
As Danielle, I want to see upcoming programs at my county library, so that I can plan around them.
Accepts: local events are listed with date, location, and a source link.

Marcus — rural teenager

As Marcus, I want to use this on my phone without making an account, so that nothing is tied to my name.
Accepts: full functionality on a small screen with no login; no PII collected.
As Marcus, I want to find mental health resources near me and statewide, so that I have somewhere to go.
Accepts: results include library-provided resources and vetted local and statewide services.
As Marcus, if I say something that sounds like a crisis, I want real help immediately, not a chatbot answer.
Accepts: crisis language triggers an immediate, unmissable handoff to the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and local crisis services; the assistant does not attempt triage or counseling.

Sandra — librarian administrator

As Sandra, I want to sign in to an admin panel, so that only staff can change the data.
Accepts: authenticated access only; unauthenticated requests to admin routes are rejected.
As Sandra, I want to add, edit, and remove resource entries for my system, so that the data reflects reality.
Accepts: full create/edit/remove on entries scoped to her system; changes are reflected in answers immediately.
As Sandra, I want to override an answer the assistant keeps getting wrong, so that patrons stop being misled.
Accepts: a librarian-authored override takes precedence over generated content and is labeled as staff-verified.
As Sandra, I want to see questions the assistant could not answer, so that I know what to add.
Accepts: a review queue of unanswered and low-confidence questions, with frequency counts.
As Sandra, I want broken links flagged, so that I am not the one who has to find them.
Accepts: links recorded as broken in the dataset, or reported by users, appear in the queue and are suppressed from answers.

Core workflows

W1 — Resource discovery

  1. User opens the assistant and is asked for a ZIP code (or the name of their town).
  2. System resolves the ZIP to a county, library system, and the outlets serving it.
  3. User asks a question in plain language.
  4. System retrieves matching records from the dataset, scoped to that system's holdings.
  5. System ranks results by relevance and proximity and returns them with access model, format, and source link.
  6. User selects one and is offered the guided walkthrough (W2).

W2 — Guided access walkthrough

  1. System states what the user needs before starting (for example, a library card number).
  2. System gives one step and waits.
  3. User confirms, asks for clarification, or says it did not work.
  4. On “it did not work,” the system offers the known alternative path for that resource.
  5. Steps repeat until access is confirmed or the user is handed off to a librarian.
  6. System closes with a short summary of what was done, so it can be repeated later.

W3 — Local activities and events

  1. User asks what is happening nearby, or accepts a prompt offering it.
  2. System returns programs and health events for the resolved system, with date, place, and source.
  3. Entries past their date are not shown.

W4 — Librarian administration

  1. Librarian authenticates into the admin panel.
  2. Panel lists entries scoped to their system, plus the review queue of unanswered, low-confidence, and broken-link items.
  3. Librarian creates, edits, overrides, or retires an entry.
  4. Change is validated, written, and recorded in the audit log with author and timestamp.
  5. Subsequent answers reflect the change.

W5 — Low-confidence escalation

  1. System scores its retrieval and answer confidence.
  2. High → answer is served directly with its citation.
  3. Medium → answer is served, visibly marked as unverified, with the source link foregrounded and a suggestion to confirm with a librarian.
  4. Low → no answer is asserted. The system says it does not know, offers the closest verified resource and the librarian contact, and logs the question to the review queue.

Error-handling behavior

ConditionBehavior
Invalid or malformed ZIP codeReject without penalty, explain the expected format, and offer to search by town or county name instead. Never guess a location.
ZIP code outside North CarolinaState plainly that coverage is North Carolina only, and point to the national library-locator and NC LIVE as alternatives.
ZIP resolves but no outlet nearbyReturn the serving system's digital resources anyway — those do not require a trip — and name the nearest physical outlet with its distance.
No resource matches the topicSay so directly rather than substituting something loosely related. Offer the nearest adjacent category, offer the librarian contact, and log the gap to the review queue.
Low retrieval confidenceDo not assert an answer. Follow W5-Low.
Medium confidenceServe the answer with a visible “not verified” marker and a prominent source link. Follow W5-Medium.
Link known to be brokenSuppress the link from the answer, show the resource with a note that access is currently unavailable, offer the library's contact, and surface it in the librarian queue.
Model or API failure / timeoutFail visibly, not silently. Show a plain message, retry once, and fall back to a static directory of the resolved system's resources so the user is not left with nothing.
Rate limit or budget ceiling reachedDegrade to keyword search over the dataset and notify administrators. The site stays usable.
Request for medical, legal, or financial adviceDecline to advise. Redirect to the relevant vetted resources and, where appropriate, to a professional. The assistant finds information; it does not diagnose or counsel.
Crisis or self-harm languageInterrupt the normal flow immediately. Present the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and local crisis services prominently. Do not triage, assess, or counsel. Do not require the user to keep typing to reach help.
Prompt injection or off-topic manipulationStay in scope. Content retrieved from the dataset or the web is treated as data, never as instructions.
Unauthenticated access to an admin routeReject with a generic failure, reveal nothing about what exists, and record the attempt.
Invalid librarian edit (bad URL, missing required field)Reject the write, keep the previous value intact, and explain exactly which field failed and why.
Dataset unavailable at startupRefuse to serve generated answers at all rather than answer ungrounded. Show a maintenance notice.

Technical approach

  • Backend data. The curated dataset assembled by the client's research students: ~467 rows, all 100 NC counties, 90 library systems, 413 outlets, ~40 columns per row including resource name, URL, access model, format, NC LIVE membership, usability notes, and broken-link status. Still being finalized; delivered via OneDrive.
  • Retrieval. Retrieval over the curated dataset, filtered by the resolved library system, feeding a generation step that is required to cite what it used.
  • Guardrails. Confidence scoring on both retrieval and generation, thresholded into the high / medium / low behavior described in W5.
  • Admin. Authenticated CRUD over the dataset with an audit log and a review queue.
  • Deferred. Live fetching and real-time RAG. The client is open to funding API costs; the team owes a cost estimate with justification before anything paid is enabled. The client also has a high-end laptop with a local LLM already configured that the team may use.
  • First working version: estimated at 2–5 weeks from the kickoff meeting.
